# Security Quick Reference: Executives
<!-- MedDefense Health Systems | Information Security -->
**Version:** 1.0 | **Date:** 2026-06-06 | **Audience:** C-suite, executive assistants, board office

---

## Your biggest threats

- **Business email compromise targeting your authority.** Three finance executives at peer healthcare organizations received wire fraud requests in Q1 2026 using the same pattern: a message appearing to come from a senior colleague, referencing a real vendor relationship, asking for a payment or routing change to bypass normal AP controls — with a confidentiality instruction to prevent peer verification. The attacker does not need you to click anything. They need you to reply, forward, or initiate. Signal to watch for: any financial or document request that instructs you not to use standard process or not to consult your team.

- **Spear phishing using your name or role against your staff.** Attackers impersonate executives to reach finance, procurement, and executive assistants — not you directly. Your name, your travel schedule, your communication style, and your organizational relationships are visible enough to make a fabricated message convincing. Signal to watch for: a staff member mentioning an unusual request they received "from you" that you did not send.

- **Credential compromise with operational reach.** An executive account that is compromised gives an attacker access to board communications, financial authorizations, and personnel matters — not just email. The March 2026 MedDefense incident began at the clinical layer but reached privileged systems within hours. An executive account compromise is a faster path to the same outcome. Signal to watch for: login alerts from unfamiliar locations or devices, or access notifications you did not initiate.

---

## What to do right now

- **Establish a verbal confirmation rule for your team this week.** Tell your assistant and any finance contacts who act on your behalf: any request involving money, credentials, or confidential documents that arrives by email requires a brief call to your known number before action is taken. This rule applies even when the email looks exactly like you wrote it.

- **Turn on login notifications for your MedDefense account and your personal accounts used for travel or board communication.** If you receive an alert for a session you did not initiate, call ext. 4-SECURITY immediately — do not reply to any email asking you to verify the login yourself.

- **Review what your assistant can authorize without your direct confirmation.** If they can initiate payments, approve documents, or grant calendar access without a secondary check, tighten that scope. Executive assistants are the second-highest-value BEC target after finance staff.

---

## When something looks wrong

**Call ext. 4-SECURITY directly.** Do not reply to the suspicious message, do not forward it to ask a colleague if it looks real, and do not click any link in it to investigate. Call the security team first. If a financial action has already been initiated, call immediately — time is the variable that determines whether a wire transfer can be recalled.

---

## What not to do

- **Do not authorize any payment, routing change, or sensitive document release by replying to an email thread.** Reply-to addresses can be silently redirected to an attacker's mailbox. Confirm by calling a known number. The email thread is not a secure confirmation channel.

- **Do not conduct board or executive business on personal email, personal devices, or hotel WiFi without VPN.** Executive communications accessed over unsecured networks are visible to anyone on that network. Use the MedDefense VPN before opening work systems on travel.

- **Do not assume urgency and confidentiality together are legitimate.** Every documented BEC attempt uses both. A request that is both time-sensitive and requires secrecy is a red flag, not a reason to move faster.
