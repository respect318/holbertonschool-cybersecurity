# What the Bank Would Have Seen — Audit Trail Reconstruction

## Scope of this review

This report reconstructs, from the `/api/v2/audit/events` and
`/api/v2/monitoring/alerts` endpoints alone (i.e. what an investigator with
only application-level visibility would have), how much of the F01–F12
chain would have been visible after the fact, correctly attributed to the
actor who performed it, and actually surfaced as an alert.

Query used throughout: `GET /api/v2/audit/events` (own session), cross-
referenced against every money-moving or privilege-changing step performed
during the engagement.

## What the audit trail actually contains

Across the full chain — deprecated-surface reads, identifier enumeration,
mass assignment, transfer amendment, reversal replay, the balance race,
and the full payment flow — the audit log recorded exactly two kinds of
event for the entire engagement:

```json
{"action": "login", "actor": "20001041", "severity": "info", "detail": "session opened"}
{"action": "segment_change", "actor": "20001041", "severity": "high", "detail": "segment changed to PRIVATE"}
```

Nothing else appears. This is the entire basis for the three failure modes
below.

---

## Failure mode 1 — Unlogged

The following money-moving or data-disclosing actions produced **no audit
event at all**, despite each one being either a direct financial operation
or a disclosure of another customer's financial data:

- `GET /api/v1/customers/{id}` and `GET /api/v1/accounts/{iban}` —
  reading another customer's profile, balance, and card PAN through the
  deprecated surface (F01/F02/F04). The victim's own timeline would show
  nothing; the bank would have no record that this data was ever accessed
  by anyone other than the account owner.
- `POST /api/v2/beneficiaries/verify` — the checksum-oracle enumeration
  used to discover live account identifiers (F03). A sweep of hundreds of
  candidate IBANs left no trace.
- `PATCH /api/v2/transfers/{id}` (post-authorization amount amendment,
  F06) and `POST /api/v2/transfers/{id}/reverse` (replayed reversal,
  F07) — both directly move money and both are silent in the log.
- The concurrent `/validate` dispatch that produced a negative balance
  (F08) — a balance driven below zero left no distinguishable event from
  an ordinary validation.
- `POST /securecheck/api/authenticate` and `POST /checkout/authorize` —
  the entire payment chain, including the final €4,800 authorization on a
  card that was never the tester's own, produced no entry in
  `/api/v2/audit/events`.

**Conclusion:** every step in the chain that actually moves money or
discloses another customer's data is unlogged. Only two non-financial,
self-referential actions (logging in, changing one's own profile field)
are ever recorded.

---

## Failure mode 2 — Mis-attributed

Because the only events that exist are scoped to the acting session's own
`actor` (`20001041`, the tester's own customer number), there is a
structural attribution gap: the audit trail has no concept of "this actor
acted on someone else's data or funds." An event log entry for reading
`GET /api/v1/customers/1087` — had one existed — would need to name **two**
principals (the reader and the record's owner) to be useful for
attribution; the schema observed only ever carries a single `actor` field
tied to the session holder, with no `subject`/`target` field distinguishing
"acted on my own record" from "acted on someone else's." Victim 1087's own
audit history, if queried, would show no indication that their card was
ever used in a transaction they did not initiate — the log has nowhere to
put that fact even in principle.

---

## Failure mode 3 — Logged but never alerted

The one event that **was** logged with an elevated severity —
`segment_change`, `severity: "high"` — never produced a corresponding
entry in `/api/v2/monitoring/alerts`, which returned `{"alerts": []}`
immediately after the event was recorded and for the remainder of the
session. The severity tagging shows the system was designed to recognize
this class of event as notable; the alert pipeline simply never converts
that recognition into anything an analyst would see. This is the clearest
evidence of a detection gap that is not a visibility gap: the data existed,
labeled as significant, and was never surfaced.

---

## The single event that would have broken the chain earliest

`segment_change` (`RETAIL → PRIVATE`, via mass assignment on
`PATCH /api/v2/customers/me`) is both the earliest **logged** event in the
full attack chain and the only one carrying a severity high enough to
justify an alert. Every step before it (surface mapping, identifier
enumeration) is reconnaissance with no logged trace; every step after it
(payment authentication, high-value authorization) is unlogged entirely.
Had the alerting pipeline actually fired on this one `high`-severity event,
an analyst would have had a chance to intervene *before* the payment flow
was ever reached — this is the earliest point at which the bank's own
instrumentation had the information needed to break the chain, and the
point at which it failed to act on it.

---

## Summary

| Failure mode | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Unlogged | v1 reads, IBAN enumeration, transfer amendment, reversal replay, balance race, entire payment flow — zero audit events |
| Mis-attributed | Audit schema carries only a single self-referential `actor`; no field exists to record "acted on another principal's data/funds" |
| Logged but never alerted | `segment_change`, severity `high`, never appears in `/api/v2/monitoring/alerts` |
| Earliest breakable point | `segment_change` — the only high-severity, logged event preceding the payment flow |
