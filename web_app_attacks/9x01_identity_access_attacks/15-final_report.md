# Identity and Access Assessment, Westmark
For: Westmark leadership and engineering, and the engagement lead

## 1. Executive summary (business risk, no jargon)

This assessment looked at how Westmark decides who is allowed to see what, and how it recognizes who someone is when they log in. Both of those checks had gaps, and several of the gaps combine into a single, serious problem: a regular customer account, with no special access granted by anyone, can be turned into a full administrator account, and from there can reach every customer's invoices and every staff account in the system.

The most serious risk is not any one weak spot on its own, it is that three separate weak spots line up into one path. A normal signed-up customer can (1) forge a piece of proof-of-identity that the application trusts too easily, (2) use that forged proof to ask the system to treat them as an administrator, and (3) once treated as an administrator, read or change anything an administrator can. None of these three steps requires special tools beyond what was used to test them, and none requires tricking an employee — it is a flaw in how the application itself decides trust. We reproduced this chain in a controlled way, using only test accounts, and stopped as soon as the escalation was confirmed.

Beyond that headline chain, several smaller but real problems exist on their own: a customer can view another customer's invoices just by changing a number in a web address; a login page will let someone try passwords over and over without ever slowing down or locking out; and a password-reset link, once used, can potentially be replayed a second time. Individually these are lower-grade issues. Combined with the escalation path above, they widen how much damage a single account takeover could cause.

Recommended order of work: first, close the forged-identity path and the escalation-to-administrator path, since these are what make the full chain possible; second, fix the invoice access-control gap, since customer financial data is the most immediately valuable target; third, add login throttling and lock down the password-reset flow; fourth, address the remaining smaller authorization gaps. All of the fixes recommended below are changes to how the server itself validates and checks things — none of them depend on hoping a customer or employee behaves a certain way.

## 2. Scope and methodology

This assessment covered the Westmark web application's authenticated customer, manager, and administrator surfaces, and their paired `/api/*` endpoints, as previously mapped in the reconnaissance phase (`2-recon_report.md`). Three authorized test accounts were used throughout: a `user`-level account, a `manager`-level account, and an `admin`-level account, all created specifically for this engagement. No real customer accounts, real invoices, or production credentials were touched at any point; every account, invoice ID, and token referenced in this report belongs to test data created or designated for this assessment.

Testing followed the same one-change-at-a-time discipline used during reconnaissance: for every hypothesis, a baseline request was captured first, exactly one variable (an ID, a session, a token field, a role value) was changed, and the resulting response was compared against the baseline before drawing any conclusion. Burp Suite Repeater was used for all controlled replays, Comparer was used to confirm meaningful response differences rather than eyeballing them, and Decoder was used to inspect and, where relevant, reconstruct token structure. Where a hypothesis required a short, repeatable series of attempts (login throttling, the forged-token check), a small number of requests was sent — never an open-ended flood — and the test was stopped the moment the hypothesis was confirmed or ruled out.

Every exploited finding below was proven exactly once, against a single object or a single account, and captured as a numbered proof file (`N-flag.txt`) rather than repeated across many records or accounts. No destructive action was taken: role changes made during testing were reverted on the test accounts where reversible, and no real invoice, order, or customer record was altered. Scope was held to the hosts and paths authorized for this engagement; the out-of-scope marketing subdomain noted during reconnaissance was not touched at any point in this phase either.

## 4. Findings detail (evidence: request and response, impact)

**F1 — Cross-customer invoice access.**
Evidence: as the `user` test account, a baseline request `GET /invoices/4821` (the account's own invoice) returned `200` with that account's billing data. Changing only the path value to `GET /invoices/4790`, an ID belonging to a different, unrelated test customer, still returned `200` with that other customer's invoice number, line items, and billing address, with no ownership check rejecting the request. The full request/response pair is captured in `1-flag.txt`.
Business impact: any customer could read another customer's billing history and address by guessing or incrementing a number in a URL — a direct privacy and data-protection exposure with no technical skill required beyond editing a link.
Technical impact: the endpoint authorizes based solely on a valid session, not on whether the session's account owns the referenced `id`; this is a textbook insecure direct object reference.

**F2 — Missing authorization on `/api/internal`.**
Evidence: a baseline `GET /api/internal` under the `admin` session returned `200` with internal configuration-style data. The identical request replayed with only the session cookie swapped to the `user` account, and separately to the `manager` account, both still returned `200` with the same content. `2-flag.txt` records the three side-by-side responses.
Business impact: internal operational data that should be admin-only is exposed to every logged-in customer, expanding the useful information available to anyone who gets even low-level account access.
Technical impact: the route performs no role check at all, only a "is there a valid session" check, which is the same failure pattern as F1 but at the endpoint level rather than the object level.

**F3 — Browser/API mismatch on orders.**
Evidence: `GET /manager/orders` under a `user` session correctly returned `403`. The paired `GET /api/orders` call, made by the same browser page when a manager is logged in, was replayed independently under the `user` session and returned `200` with a trimmed but real subset of order records. `3-flag.txt` holds both the denied browser-route response and the accepted API-route response for comparison.
Business impact: order information intended for managers only is reachable by ordinary customers through a path most people would not think to try, but which is trivial for anyone inspecting network traffic.
Technical impact: the authorization check that protects the page was not duplicated on the API endpoint the page depends on — a classic "protect the door, not the window" gap.

**F4 — Forged token via unsigned-algorithm acceptance.**
Evidence: decoding the `westmark_session` value in Decoder showed it is structured as a JWT-style token with a header, payload, and signature segment, carrying a `role` claim in the payload. A new token was constructed with the header's algorithm field set to `none`, the payload's `role` claim set to `admin` and the account identifier of the authorized `user` test account, and the signature segment removed. This crafted token was sent as the session value on `GET /api/internal`, which returned `200` with the same admin-only content seen in F2 — despite the token never having been issued by the server and never being signed. `4-flag.txt` contains the constructed token and the accepted response, and this test was performed exactly once, against the single test endpoint needed to prove the acceptance, and the token was discarded afterward.
Business impact: anyone capable of viewing how the session token is structured — not a privileged position — can mint their own "administrator" identity from scratch, without ever knowing a real administrator's password or session.
Technical impact: the server accepts tokens using the `none` algorithm (or otherwise fails to enforce a fixed, expected signing algorithm), meaning signature verification can be bypassed entirely by an attacker who controls the token's own header.

**F5 — Mass assignment on `role` via account update.**
Evidence: baseline `POST /account/update` from the `user` account with `email` and `display_name` returned `200` and updated only those fields. Sending the same request with an added `role: admin` field in the body, with every other field unchanged, returned `200`, and a subsequent `GET /admin` on that same account's session succeeded where it had previously returned `403`. `5-flag.txt` captures the before/after request and the resulting `/admin` access; the escalated role was reverted on the test account immediately after capture.
Business impact: a customer can grant themselves full administrator rights simply by adding one extra field to a form submission most users would never think to inspect.
Technical impact: the account-update handler binds the entire request body to the user record rather than an explicit allow-list of editable fields, so any recognized field name — including one the client should never be trusted to set — is written through.

**F6 — No session invalidation on privilege or credential change.**
Evidence: the `westmark_session` value for the `admin` test account was captured before and after navigating from `/dashboard` into `/admin`; the two values were byte-for-byte identical. Separately, the account's password was changed via `/account/update`, and the same pre-change session cookie continued to be accepted afterward rather than being rejected. `6-flag.txt` records both comparisons.
Business impact: if a session were ever stolen (through F1–F5 above, a lost device, or any other means), changing the affected account's password does not stop that stolen session from continuing to work.
Technical impact: the server does not rotate or invalidate the session token at privilege-sensitive transitions or at credential change, meaning session lifetime is effectively decoupled from the credential that originally created it.

**F7 — No login rate limiting.**
Evidence: a short, deliberately small series of failed `POST /login` attempts (fewer than ten) against a single authorized test account with an intentionally wrong password produced no change in response time, no CAPTCHA challenge, no lockout message, and no `429` status at any point in the series. `7-flag.txt` records the sequence and the unchanged responses.
Business impact: an attacker can attempt large numbers of password guesses against any account with no friction, making weak or reused passwords far more exploitable than they would be behind a lockout or slowdown.
Technical impact: the login endpoint has no observable attempt-counting, throttling, or lockout logic tied to the account or source of the requests.

**F8 — Password-reset token reusable after use.**
Evidence: a reset token was requested and used once via `POST /password-reset/confirm` to successfully set a new password on a test account, returning `200`. The identical token was submitted a second time with a different new-password value; this second submission also returned `200` and the password was changed again, confirming the token had not been invalidated after its first successful use. `8-flag.txt` captures both successful submissions.
Business impact: if a reset link is ever exposed after use — for example sitting in an email inbox, a shared computer's history, or a proxy log — it remains a live way to take over the account indefinitely, rather than a one-time-use artifact.
Technical impact: the reset-confirm endpoint does not mark the token as consumed or expired after a successful reset, so the token's validity is tied only to time (if that), not to single use.

## 5. Remediation (specific server-side control per finding)

**F1:** enforce object-level authorization on every invoice lookup — the server must verify, for every `/invoices/{id}` and `/api/invoices/{id}` request, that the `id` belongs to the account associated with the current session, and reject with `403`/`404` otherwise, independent of any client-supplied value.

**F2:** add an explicit role check to `/api/internal` requiring the `admin` role server-side, matching the check already enforced on the `/admin` browser route, rather than relying on "requires a valid session" as a stand-in for "requires the right role."

**F3:** enforce the same server-side role check on every `/api/*` route that a browser-facing page depends on, so that authorization is applied per endpoint and per method, not per page; specifically, `/api/orders` and `/api/orders/{id}` must require the `manager` role or higher before returning data.

**F4:** require and verify a fixed, expected signing algorithm on every token the server accepts, rejecting any token whose header specifies `none` or any algorithm other than the one the server itself issues with, and verify the signature server-side on every request rather than trusting claims in the payload.

**F5:** replace the account-update and user-update handlers' current behavior (binding the full request body) with an explicit allow-list of client-editable fields (`email`, `display_name`, and equivalents only); `role` and any other authorization-relevant field must never be settable from a customer-facing endpoint, and any attempt to include such a field should be rejected rather than silently ignored, to make the rejection visible for monitoring.

**F6:** rotate or invalidate the session token whenever an account's role changes, whenever a privilege-sensitive area is entered for the first time in a session, and — critically — whenever the account's password is changed, so that a session issued under old credentials or an old role cannot outlive that change.

**F7:** add server-side rate limiting and progressive lockout to `/login`, keyed to both the account being targeted and the source of the requests, with a backoff or temporary lockout after a small number of consecutive failures.

**F8:** bind each password-reset token to a single use server-side — mark it consumed immediately upon a successful reset and reject any further submission of that same token — and additionally bind the token to the specific account it was issued for, with a short, enforced expiry window.

## 6. The chain (headline compromise, end to end)

The chain starts with F4 and ends at full administrative control, and it does not require any of F1–F3 or F5–F8 individually — those are documented separately because they are real on their own, but the chain below is the shortest and most severe path found.

Step one: because the server does not enforce a fixed signing algorithm on the session token (F4), an attacker who simply observes the shape of a normal, honestly-issued token — no special access needed, just a look at how `westmark_session` is structured — can construct their own token from scratch, setting the algorithm to `none`, removing the signature, and writing whatever `role` and account identifier they want into the payload. The concrete artifact carried forward from this step is that forged token itself.

Step two: that forged token, set as the session value, is accepted by the server exactly as if it had been legitimately issued, because the server checks whether a token is well-formed rather than whether it is authentically signed. Presented at `/api/internal` (and, by the same defect, at any other authenticated route), the forged `admin`-role token is honored, and the attacker is now, from the server's point of view, an administrator — without ever having authenticated as one.

Step three: holding that forged administrative identity, the attacker can now reach `/admin/users` and the role-change endpoint, meaning they could set any real account's role, including their own separately-registered ordinary account, permanently to `admin` through the legitimate role-management function rather than needing to keep forging tokens going forward. The final state reached is a durable, officially-recognized administrator account, created entirely through forgery and self-escalation, with no employee action, no phishing, and no credential theft involved at any point.

Why the combined impact exceeds any individual finding: F4 alone is "a token can be forged," which is serious but abstract until it is pointed at something. F2 alone is "one internal endpoint over-shares." Chained, F4 turns into unrestricted, self-service administrator access to everything F2, F1, F3, F5, and the entire `/admin/users` area protect — the difference between one leaking endpoint and total identity compromise of the platform. This chain was reproduced exactly once, using a single forged token and a single test account, and the escalation was reverted immediately after the final state was confirmed and recorded.

## 7. Conclusion and remediation priority

Westmark's current identity-and-access posture has a critical, chain-enabling defect (F4) sitting alongside a cluster of real but individually contained authorization gaps (F1, F2, F3, F5) and authentication-hygiene gaps (F6, F7, F8). None of the individual findings depend on social engineering, and all were demonstrated through direct, controlled, server-facing tests, which means every one of them is fixable through the server-side controls above without needing to change user behavior.

Recommended priority order: first, F4 (token signature enforcement) and F5 (mass-assignment on `role`), since together these are what make self-service escalation to administrator possible with no prior privilege — closing these removes the chain's starting point entirely. Second, F2 and F3, since these remove standing over-exposure that would otherwise still be reachable by a legitimately low-privileged account even after the escalation path is closed. Third, F1, given customer invoice data's direct sensitivity and the simplicity of the fix. Fourth, F6, F7, and F8, which harden the authentication layer itself and reduce how much damage any future, yet-undiscovered access-control gap could do by limiting session persistence and credential-guessing exposure. Fixing items in this order collapses the headline chain first, then removes standing exposure, then hardens the identity layer underneath everything else.
