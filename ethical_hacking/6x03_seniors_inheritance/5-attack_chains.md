# Attack Chain Analysis

**Engagement:** MediPath Diagnostics (VS-MPD-26-041)
**Basis:** This analysis works only with the findings retained after triage in `4-findings_triage.md` — F-01/F-10 (8.8 High), F-02 (6.7 Medium), F-03/F-09 (9.1 Critical), F-04 (7.5 High), F-05 (8.3 High), F-06 (8.3 High), and F-08 (4.6 Medium). F-07 is excluded as retired. Each chain below sequences findings the way an attacker would use them in order, not merely because they share a root cause, and states an outcome that none of the individual findings produces alone.

## Chain 1: Integration-credential persistence to cross-tenant health-data exposure

- **Sequence:** F-03/F-09 → F-01/F-10 → F-05
- **Path:** An attacker who obtains or misuses the long-lived, over-scoped hospital connector token (F-03) gains a foothold inside the Hospital Integration Gateway that is not limited to its assigned hospital relationship. Because authorization state across MediPath's identity and session infrastructure is revoked on a time-based schedule rather than at the moment of a triggering event (the same gap documented in F-09), that foothold — or an equivalent administrative session obtained through it — persists usefully past the point MediPath would expect it to have been cut off, giving the attacker a stable operating window rather than a one-shot access. From that position, the attacker pivots into the tenant-identity enforcement gap shared by the Results API and the internal analytics service (F-01/F-10): since neither service independently re-verifies tenant ownership once past the gateway, the attacker walks diagnostic result records and aggregate laboratory activity across multiple tenants rather than the single hospital relationship the original token was scoped to. Finally, holding a foothold inside the integration path, the attacker uses the sample-status webhook's lack of message-level authentication (F-05) to inject falsified status transitions — network position inside the integration boundary makes the source-IP allowlist irrelevant as a barrier.
- **Consolidated impact:** No single finding in this sequence produces this outcome alone. F-03 alone is a scope-and-lifetime credential problem confined to one hospital connector; F-01/F-10 alone is a tenant-isolation gap that still requires the attacker to already be an authenticated user; F-05 alone is a single forged status event. Chained, they produce a durable, multi-laboratory compromise: sustained unauthorized access to diagnostic result records across an unbounded number of MediPath's 400 laboratory tenants, combined with the ability to falsify sample-status data that downstream result-release and export workflows may trust. For a healthcare-data processor preparing for an HDS renewal audit, this is not a technical inconvenience — it is the kind of cross-tenant, integrity-and-confidentiality-compromising incident that triggers DPO notification analysis, laboratory-client communication, and potential certification-evidence questions simultaneously, which is a materially larger and more regulator-visible event than any one finding's remediation note suggests.

## Chain 2: Support-channel enumeration to bulk export exfiltration

- **Sequence:** F-08 → F-02 → F-06
- **Path:** This chain does not require a privileged credential or a network-boundary breach; it lives entirely inside functionality available to a low-privilege actor, which is why it is easy to miss when each finding is scored in isolation. The password-recovery response difference (F-08) lets an attacker cheaply confirm which laboratory-user email addresses are valid MediPath accounts, without needing to compromise any of them directly. Armed with a list of confirmed, active support-adjacent identities, the attacker targets one of those accounts with a case containing the stored, unencoded content documented in F-02 — since support analysts routinely open cases from multiple laboratory tenants, a single successful stored-content execution against one analyst session yields a same-origin foothold that is not confined to the laboratory that submitted the case. From that foothold, rather than attempting a high-friction bulk export (the DPO-gated F-07 path, which is retired here precisely because it is well controlled), the attacker uses the far quieter path of retrieving or regenerating patient/laboratory export packages and relying on the retention mismatch in F-06: because signed export links remain valid for roughly 30 days instead of the documented 24 hours, the attacker has a wide operational window to retrieve health-data exports without needing to act immediately or repeat the noisier enumeration and support-portal steps.
- **Consolidated impact:** Individually, F-08 is a low-friction reconnaissance nuisance, F-02 is a same-origin execution with no credential retained in Sarah's proof, and F-06 is a retention-policy mismatch on data that is still encrypted at rest. None of the three, alone, describes a realistic path to bulk health-data exfiltration. Combined, they describe exactly that: targeted identification of real accounts, a low-noise same-origin compromise of a session that spans multiple laboratory tenants, and a wide, quietly available window to pull export packages that MediPath's own policy assumed would already be gone. This is the chain most likely to escape a per-finding review, because each step reads as medium severity on its own — the combined outcome is a sustained, support-channel exfiltration path for regulated health data that never needs to touch MediPath's highest-privilege systems, which arguably makes it *harder* to detect through controls aimed at privileged-access misuse.

## Notes on scope

- Both chains are built exclusively from findings retained after the Task 4 triage; F-07 does not appear because it was retired as documented, authorized functionality, not because it was overlooked.
- Chain 1 is the sequence a careful reader reaches by simply following the highest-severity retained findings in order; Chain 2 is presented with appropriate restraint — it depends on a specific, plausible sequence of actions (enumerate, compromise a support session, exploit a retention window) rather than an assumed guaranteed outcome, and its value is in showing that the medium-severity findings are not independent noise even though no single one of them reaches a high severity rating on its own.
- Other combinations among the retained findings (for example, F-04's connector-worker TLS gap alongside F-03/F-09) were considered but are not presented as a chain here because the managed mTLS gateway's independent certificate validation, evidenced in the recalibration, breaks the plausible path between them without further confirmation of a bypass — a genuine gap is not the same as a demonstrable pivot.
