# Strategic Threat Profile, Nordstrøm Power Group

## Framework choice and rationale
This profile uses a hybrid lens: MITRE ATT&CK (Enterprise) for corporate IT, trading, and administrative surfaces, and MITRE ATT&CK for ICS for the generation, offshore, and hydrogen control environments, with a PASTA-style business/regulatory-impact overlay driving the severity and regulatory columns. A single-framework approach does not fit Nordstrøm: the group spans five subsidiaries with different IT/OT ratios (pure trading and administrative systems in Denmark's market-facing arm versus safety-critical OT in Norwegian offshore gas and Dutch hydrogen), different national regulators, and one subsidiary — the German solar business — mid-integration, where the relevant threats are less "external technique" and more "residual pre-acquisition exposure." The hybrid structure lets the same document carry a technically precise IT/OT technique mapping while still surfacing the business-continuity and regulatory-reporting stakes the board actually cares about.

## Group-level profile
As a Scandinavian-headquartered, multi-country energy holding, Nordstrøm's group-level exposure centers on five recurring threat lines: (1) state-linked actors with a sabotage or intelligence interest in European energy security, most relevant where OT directly controls physical production or grid-connected assets; (2) ransomware groups treating energy operators as high-leverage targets because production downtime is costly and safety-sensitive, particularly at IT/OT convergence points such as historians and engineering workstations; (3) supply-chain and vendor-access risk, since OT environments across the group rely on OEM remote maintenance links; (4) insider and residual-access risk, concentrated in Germany because of the incomplete post-acquisition integration; and (5) hacktivist activity tied to energy-transition politics (hydro, offshore wind, hydrogen), which mainly threatens public-facing and reputational assets rather than core OT. Regulatory pressure is a genuine differentiator, not a footnote: all five subsidiaries fall under some national transposition of NIS2 (or an EEA-harmonized equivalent for Norway), but the essential-entity classification, competent authority, and reporting clock differ subsidiary by subsidiary, which is why the regulatory-dimension column below is written per country rather than as one "EU energy sector" line.

## Subsidiary sub-profiles

### Norway (offshore gas)

| Threat actor | Asset exposure | Technique relevance | Severity | Regulatory dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| State-linked actor with sabotage/energy-security motive | Engineering workstations and PLCs controlling offshore gas separation and export trains | T0822 (External Remote Services): OEM/vendor remote maintenance links into offshore engineering workstations are a plausible entry point; an actor using stolen VPN credentials to reach an OT jump host can then pivot to process control, which is why this actor-asset pairing is severity-relevant rather than generic | High | Norway sits outside the EU but harmonizes via the EEA; incident reporting runs through the Petroleum Safety Authority (Ptil) and national offshore safety regime, distinct from mainland NIS2 competent-authority channels |
| Ransomware group | IT/OT DMZ historian aggregating offshore production data | T1486 (Data Encrypted for Impact): encrypting the historian forces manual production reporting on a continuously operating offshore platform, giving the actor real extortion leverage rather than a nuisance outage | High | Ptil offshore incident-notification duties, plus group-level NIS2-equivalent essential-entity reporting |
| Contractor with legitimate HMI access | Platform control-room HMI and setpoints | T0836 (Modify Parameter): a contractor altering control setpoints during a routine maintenance window is a credible insider path with direct safety consequence, distinct from an external-attacker technique | Medium | Norwegian Working Environment Act plus petroleum-sector safety-incident reporting |

### Sweden (hydro and wind)

| Threat actor | Asset exposure | Technique relevance | Severity | Regulatory dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hacktivist opposed to hydro/wind siting | Internet-facing SCADA gateway for dam-level and wind-output monitoring | T1190 (Exploit Public-Facing Application): the monitoring dashboard is the one OT-adjacent asset with a public attack surface, making it the realistic target for defacement or denial rather than deep process manipulation | Medium | Swedish Civil Contingencies Agency (MSB), the national NIS2 competent authority for energy |

| Ransomware group | Corporate IT sharing an Active Directory forest with the SCADA historian domain | T1078 (Valid Accounts): credential reuse between corporate AD and the OT historian domain is the specific lateral-movement path that turns a routine IT ransomware incident into an OT-adjacent one | High | Swedish Electricity Act incident-reporting obligations |

|---|---|---|---|---|

### Germany (integration)

| Threat actor | Asset exposure | Technique relevance | Severity | Regulatory dimension |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opportunistic ransomware actor | Pre-acquisition IT stack not yet migrated onto group SOC/EDR coverage | T1190 (Exploit Public-Facing Application): legacy internet-facing systems retained from before the acquisition and not yet inventoried by the group security function are the specific reason Germany's IT exposure is higher than the other subsidiaries' comparably sized IT estates | High | German BSI IT-Sicherheitsgesetz 2.0 and the German NIS2 transposition (NIS2UmsuCG), which treats solar generation as part of the energy critical-infrastructure sector |
| Actor exploiting residual pre-acquisition access | Former-owner administrator accounts not yet deprovisioned | T1078 (Valid Accounts): incomplete offboarding of legacy administrator accounts during M&A integration is a well-documented residual-access pattern, and it is the single clearest reason Germany needs a differentiated, integration-aware threat view rather than a generic subsidiary template | High | German data protection authority (BfDI) plus works-council co-determination requirements on IT and monitoring changes |
| Supply-chain actor via inherited vendor relationships | Solar-plant monitoring and inverter-management platforms on the pre-acquisition vendor stack | T0822 (External Remote Services): vendor remote-access agreements inherited from the previous owner predate any group security review, making this a distinct inherited-risk technique rather than a newly introduced one | Medium-High | German KRITIS critical-infrastructure obligations, with applicability still pending formal determination post-acquisition — a gap this profile flags rather than assumes closed |

## Why this matters for scope architecture
Germany is the one subsidiary where the threat picture is dominated by transitional, integration-specific exposure — legacy internet-facing assets, un-deprovisioned admin accounts, and inherited vendor access — rather than steady-state OT or trading risk. That distinction argues for treating Germany as a scope decision point later in the engagement (independent validation rather than reliance on pre-acquisition audit evidence, and possible sequencing after the other four subsidiaries), not as a sixth line item in an undifferentiated matrix. Norway and the Netherlands carry the group's highest-severity OT/safety exposure and should weight staffing toward ICS-qualified assessors; Denmark's trading desk and REMIT obligations argue for a distinct, non-OT workstream; Sweden's shared AD/historian domain is the clearest single lateral-movement finding to prioritize for remediation guidance regardless of assessment depth elsewhere.
